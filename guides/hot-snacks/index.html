Convenience store

Why Is the Best Snack Hiding Behind the Register?

You have already chosen your drink and snacks when you notice fried chicken, croquettes, and steamed buns sitting in a warm display beside the cashier.

They look close enough to choose but just far enough away to create a new question: How do I get one of those?

First Move

Point to the item in the heated display and say, “Kore o hitotsu kudasai.”

Quick steps

  1. Point to the item you want.
  2. Say, “Kore o hitotsu kudasai.”
Tip

The selection changes by store, time, and season. An empty space may simply mean that today’s popular snack was faster than you.

Say or show this

Use these phrases to speak or point when you need help.

Point to the item while saying it. Kore o hitotsu kudasai. これを一つください。 One of these, please.
Use when the item is easy to identify. Furaido chikin o hitotsu onegaishimasu. フライドチキンを一つお願いします。 One fried chicken, please.
Use when you cannot see one in the case. Nikuman wa arimasu ka? 肉まんはありますか？ Do you have nikuman?
Use when one hot snack has somehow become two. Futatsu kudasai. 二つください。 Two, please.

If You See or Hear This

Furaido chikin フライドチキン It means: Fried chicken. Your move A classic convenience-store hot snack.
Karaage からあげ It means: Japanese fried chicken. Your move Usually smaller pieces rather than one large fillet.
Nikuman 肉まん It means: Steamed bun with a savory meat filling. Your move Especially tempting on a cold day.
Korokke コロッケ It means: Croquette. Your move Usually crispy outside and soft inside.
Urikire 売り切れ It means: Sold out. Your move Choose another snack and begin a new favorite.

If You’re Stuck

Point through the display and hold up one finger. You do not need to know the product’s full Japanese name. At the hot-snack counter, pointing is not a backup plan. It is fully supported ordering technology.

Tiny Culture Note

The hot display turns a convenience store into a tiny kitchen. You can walk in for a bottle of water and leave with hot fried chicken or a steamed bun you had not planned to meet. That is part of the danger—and the charm—of the Japanese konbini.
