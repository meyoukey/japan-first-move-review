What to Do When a Japanese Restaurant Says “We’re Full”

If a restaurant in Japan says it is full, it can be hard to know what they really mean.

Sometimes it means you can wait. Sometimes it means they cannot take more customers today. If the answer is unclear, it may be easier to look for another option nearby.

First Move

Ask once if waiting is possible, then move on if the answer is unclear.

Quick steps

  1. Step away from the entrance so you are not blocking others.
  2. Ask if waiting is possible.
  3. If staff gives you a clear wait time, decide if you want to wait.
  4. If the answer is unclear, it may be easier to look for another option nearby.
  5. Thank them and find another option nearby.

Don’t Do This

Don’t keep asking again and again.

If staff avoids giving a wait time or says it is difficult, they may not be able to seat you.

Don’t stand directly in the doorway while deciding.

Small restaurants often have narrow entrances. Step aside first, then check your next option.

Say or show this

Use these phrases to speak or point when you need help.

Use when you want to ask if waiting is okay. Matte mo ii desu ka? 待ってもいいですか？ Can we wait?
Use when you want to ask how long it will take. Dono kurai kakarimasu ka? どのくらいかかりますか？ How long will it take?

If You See or Hear This

Manseki desu. 満席です。 It means: We’re full. Your move Ask once if you can wait.
Chotto muzukashii desu. ちょっと難しいです。 It means: It may be difficult. Your move If they do not offer a wait time, it may be better to look for another option nearby.
Goyoyaku de ippai desu. ご予約でいっぱいです。 It means: We are fully booked. Your move Thank them and leave.

If You’re Stuck

If staff gives a number like 20 minutes or 30 minutes, waiting is probably okay. If they do not give a wait time or offer a waiting option, it may be easier to look for another option nearby.

Tiny Culture Note

At small restaurants, staff may use soft language when they cannot seat more customers. If the answer stays unclear, it is usually best to look for another option nearby.
